Core Values
Core values are a set of beliefs, ideals or practices that inform how
you conduct your life, both personally and professionally.
Businesses can also have and maintain core values .
These can help an organization determine how to allocate resources,
make important decisions and grow.
22/04/2026 Prof Mayank Pareek 31
Characteristics of Core Values
•Deeply held – They are strongly believed and not easily changed.
•Guiding principles – They influence decisions, actions, and priorities.
•Consistent over time – They remain stable, even when situations change.
•Universal within a group – In organizations, they are shared and understood by members.
•Non-negotiable – They are not compromised for convenience or short-term gain.
•Action-oriented – They shape how people behave, not just what they believe.
•Identity-defining – They reflect who a person or organization truly is.
•Applicable across situations – They guide behavior in both easy and difficult circumstances.

13-01-2026 Prof Mayank Pareek 17
McGregors’ Theory X
• Theory X (Authoritarian, Pessimistic View)
• Employees dislike work and will avoid it if possible.
• People need strict supervision and control.
• Workers are motivated by punishment and monetary rewards.
• They avoid responsibility and prefer to be directed.
• Management must use coercion, control, and threats to ensure work
gets done.
13-01-2026
McGregors’ Theory Y
• Theory Y (Participative, Optimistic View)
• Employees enjoy work and see it as natural.
• People are self-motivated and capable of responsibility.
• Workers seek opportunities for growth and creativity.
• Given the right conditions, they will work towards company goals
willingly.
• Management should foster a trusting, empowering environment.

Some Basic Financial Terms
Assets ?
An asset is basically something valuable that a person, company, or
organization owns or controls, which can provide future economic
benefits.
Fixed Assets?
A fixed asset is a type of long-term asset that a company or individual
owns and uses in its operations for an extended period, usually more
than a year, and is not intended for immediate sale.
In short, it’s something physical or tangible that helps a business
generate income over time.
Characteristics of FA
Long-term use – Used for more than one accounting period (typically a year or more).
Not for resale – Unlike inventory, it’s not meant to be sold as part of normal business
Tangible or intangible – Mostly physical (land, machinery), but sometimes intangible
Depreciation applies – Fixed assets lose value over time (except land) due to wear and tear.
Examples of FA
Land and Buildings – Office, factory, warehouse
Machinery and Equipment – Production machines, printers, vehicles used for business
Furniture and Fixtures – Office desks, chairs, shelves
Computers and IT Systems – Hardware, sometimes software
Current Assets
Current assets are assets that a business expects to convert into cash,
sell, or use up within one year (or within the company’s operating
cycle, whichever is longer).
They are short-term resources that help a company manage its day-to-
day operations.
Characteristics of CA
Short-term in nature – Usually within 12 months.
Easily convertible to cash – They can be quickly sold or used.
Used for daily operations – They help fund ongoing business activities.
Examples of CA
Cash and cash equivalents – Actual cash, bank balances, short-term deposits
Accounts receivable – Money owed by customers
Inventory – Raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods
Short-term investments – Stocks, bonds, or other investments expected to be sold within a year
Prepaid expenses – Payments made in advance for services (like insurance or rent)
Tangible Assets
Tangible assets are physical assets that you can see, touch, or feel,
which are owned by a business or individual and have economic value.
They are used in operations or held as investments
Characteristics of TA
Physical form – You can physically touch or measure them.
Economic benefit – They help generate revenue or can be sold for cash.
Depreciable (usually) – Most tangible assets lose value over time (except land).
Owned and controlled – The business has legal rights to use or sell them.
Examples of TA
• Cash, Inventory, ST investment
• Buildings, Plant and M/c
• Oil Reserves, RM
• Computers, Servers
Intangible Assets
Intangible assets are non-physical assets that a business owns, which
cannot be touched or seen, but have value because they give the
business future economic benefits
Characteristics of ITA
No physical substance – You cannot touch or hold them.
Provide economic benefit – Help generate revenue or give a competitive advantage.
Can be bought, sold, or licensed – Some can be transferred.
Amortization applies – Most intangible assets lose value over time,
Examples of ITA
• Intellectual Property : Patent, Copyright
• Brand Value and Reputation : Logo, Trade Mark
• Software and Licences
• Customer Relationships, Loyalty Program
Liability
• A liability is basically a legal or financial obligation that a person or business
owes to someone else, usually as a result of borrowing money, buying
goods/services on credit, or other commitments.
• In simple terms: Liabilities are what you owe.
Characteristics of Liability
Present obligation – You are currently responsible for paying or settling it.
Arises from past events – Like borrowing money, receiving goods on credit, contracts.
Settlement usually involves money, goods, or services – Most often cash
Current Liabilities
Current liabilities are short-term financial obligations that a business
must pay within one year or within its operating cycle, whichever is
longer.
They represent what the company owes in the near future.
Characteristics of Current Liabilities
Short-term obligation – Usually due within 12 months.
Settled using current assets – Paid with cash, inventory, or other resources.
Arises from past transactions – Like purchasing goods/services on credit or borrowing ST
Examples of Current Liabilities
• Money owed to suppliers for goods or services
• Bank overdrafts, short-term borrowings
• Salaries payable, rent payable, utilities payable
• Income tax, or other government taxes due
• Payments received for goods/services not yet delivered
Long Tem Liabilities
Long-term liabilities are financial obligations or debts that a business
must pay after more than one year (or beyond the company’s normal
operating cycle). They represent money the company owes in the long
run.
Due after one year
Usually larger obligations – Often used to finance major projects or
investments.
Arise from past transactions or contracts – Like loans, bonds, or lease
agreements
Examples of LT Liabilities
• Loans & Borrowings
• Bonds Payable
• Legal Obligations
• Pensions & Retirals
• Deferred Tax Liabilities
Contingent Liabilities
• A contingent liability is a potential obligation that may arise in the
future, depending on the outcome of a certain event.
• In other words, it’s a “maybe liability” — the company might have to
pay, but it’s not certain yet
• Depends on a future event, Lawsuit
• Disclosed in Financial Statements
• Assessment needed – Companies evaluate the likelihood and amount
to determine reporting.
Examples of Contingent Liabilities

• Warranty Claims : Liability arises only if a customer claims under
warranty.
• Company guarantees a loan of another entity; liability occurs if they
default.
• Potential tax payment if a dispute is resolved unfavorably.

Non Cash Charge
• A non-cash charge is an accounting expense recorded in the books that does not
involve an actual cash outflow.
• Non-cash charge = an expense that reduces profit on paper, but no money actually
leaves the business.
• Key Features of Non-Cash Charges
• No cash payment – It’s an accounting entry, not a real payment.
• Reduces reported profit – Affects the income statement but not the cash flow
directly.
• Reflects asset usage or obligations – Usually related to depreciation, amortization,
or provisions.
Examples of Non Cash Charge
• Depreciation : Allocating the cost of machinery, buildings, or
equipment over their useful life
• Amortization : Gradual expense of intangible assets like patents or
software
• Provisions : Accounting for potential future losses (e.g., bad debt
provision)
• Stock Options : Employee stock options recorded as expense
What Is Depreciation?
It is the gradual reduction in the value of a fixed asset over time due to
use, wear and tear, or obsolescence.
In accounting, it means spreading the cost of an asset over its useful life
instead of charging the full cost in the year it was purchased.
For example, if a company buys equipment for Rs 1,000,000 expected
to last 10 years, it does not record the entire Rs 1,000,000 as an expense
immediately. Instead, it allocates the cost across 10 years.
Why Depreciation?
Assets lose value over time : Machines, vehicles, and equipment wear out or become outdated.
To show the real profit : Instead of recording the full cost in one year, spread the cost over use.
To match cost with use : If asset is used for many years, cost should be shared over those years.
For tax purposes : Depreciation reduces profit on paper, which can reduce tax.

•

Ways of Calculating Depreciation
Declining Balance Method (Reducing Balance Method)
Applies a fixed percentage to the asset’s book value each year.
✔ Higher depreciation in early years
✔ Lower depreciation in later years
✔ Suitable for assets that lose value quickly (e.g., technology)
Example Declining Balance Method
A company buys equipment for Rs 100,000.
•
Useful life = 5 years.
Depreciation rate = 20% per year (fixed rate).
In the declining balance method, we apply the percentage to the book value each year, not the original cost.
•
Year 1
•
20% of 10,000 = RS 20,000
•
Book value at end of Year 1 = 100,000 − 20,000 = $80,000
Year 2
20% of 80,000 = Rs 16,000
•
Book value at end of Year 2 = 80,000 − 16000 = 64000
Year 3
•
20% of 64000 = Rs 12800
•
Book value at end of Year 3 = 64000 − 12800 = 51200
Declining Balance Method
Key Point:
Depreciation decreases each year.
It is always calculated on the remaining book value.
Higher expense in early years, lower in later years.

• It is ability of a company or individual to quickly convert assets into
cash to meet short-term obligations without significant loss of value.
• High liquidity: Assets can be easily converted to cash (e.g., cash, bank
deposits, marketable securities).
• Low liquidity: Assets take longer to convert into cash or may lose
value (e.g., machinery, land).
• Key point:
• Liquidity focuses on short-term financial health, not long-term
profitability.
Implications of Liquidity
Solvency & Financial Safety
High liquidity ensures a company can pay its bills, salaries, and short-term debts on time.
Low liquidity increases risk of default or cash flow problems.
Investment & Growth
Companies with sufficient liquidity can take advantage of opportunities,
Creditworthiness
Lenders and suppliers check liquidity ratios before granting loans or credit.
Good liquidity → easier access to short-term credit.
Risk Management
Liquidity cushions the business during economic downturns or unexpected expenses.
Cash Flow
• Cash Flow is the movement of cash into and out of a business over a specific period.
• Inflows: Money coming in (sales revenue, loans, investments)
• Outflows: Money going out (expenses, debt repayment, asset purchases)
• Cash flow shows actual liquidity — the cash a company has to meet its obligations, unlike accounting profit
which may include non-cash items like depreciation.
• Types of Cash Flow:
• Operating Cash Flow: Cash from core business operations (selling goods/services).
• Investing Cash Flow: Cash spent or earned from buying/selling assets (machinery, land, investments).
• Financing Cash Flow: Cash from raising or repaying capital (loans, issuing shares, paying dividends).

The Bull Whip Effect
Small fluctuations in customer demand cause increasingly larger
fluctuations in orders and inventory up the supply chain.
• Minor demand changes at the retailer level → amplified impact at the
wholesaler, distributor, and manufacturer levels
• A 5% increase in demand for toilet paper at retail leads to a 20%
increase in orders at the manufacturer
Causes of Bull Whip Effect
• Demand Forecasting Errors:
• Overreacting to small changes in customer demand
• Order Batching:
• Companies place larger, less frequent orders to save costs, amplifying
variability
• Price Fluctuations & Promotions:
• Promotions cause temporary spikes, leading to over-ordering
• Lack of Information Sharing:
• Suppliers don’t see actual consumer demand, only retailer orders
Example of Bull Whip Effect
• Automotive Industry
• Sudden change in car sales demand → amplified orders for parts like
semiconductors → global shortages
Impact of Bull Whip
•Excessive inventory
•Stockouts and lost sales
•Poor customer service
•Higher logistics and storage costs
•Inefficient resource allocation

Key Traits of an Autocratic Leader:

9. Fear-Based Motivation – Uses control, punishment, or strict supervision to maintain
